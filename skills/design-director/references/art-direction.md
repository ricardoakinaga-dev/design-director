# Art direction

Art direction is the bridge between product strategy and visible choices. It defines the visual world, what belongs in it, and how assets behave together.

## Art-direction brief

Record:

- audience, context, and desired action;
- design thesis and emotional register;
- visual territory and explicit anti-territory;
- subject, setting, composition, crop, camera, light, material, and palette;
- typography and exact text treatment;
- motion and responsive behavior;
- identity locks and permitted transformations;
- asset roles, output formats, and acceptance tests.

## Build a visual grammar

Define a small grammar that can survive across the interface:

- shape language: geometric, organic, editorial, utilitarian, or hybrid;
- depth model: flat, layered, tactile, atmospheric, or spatial;
- image language: documentary, constructed, diagrammatic, illustrative, or abstract;
- type voice: quiet, technical, literary, playful, or assertive;
- contrast strategy: tonal, chromatic, typographic, spatial, or material.

Use the grammar to reject attractive but unrelated assets. A good asset can still be wrong if its light, crop, material, or cultural signal disagrees with the product.

## Variants

Create variants only to answer a decision. Conservative protects recognition; Premium increases refinement and editorial control; Bold increases memorability and risk. Keep subject and identity stable when comparing art direction. Change one meaningful axis at a time where possible so a critic can identify the cause of preference.

## Concept acceptance

An accepted concept is a specification, not a license to improvise. Record:

- elements that must survive;
- implementation translations (image, SVG, CSS, component, motion);
- details intentionally exploratory;
- known limitations such as font or asset unavailability;
- the reference screenshot or artifact path used for comparison.
